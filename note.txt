Yes. This is basically the entire image formation pipeline, from photons to the RGB pixels you see on your monitor.

I'll use a digital camera as the example.

1. Light exists as photons

A scene is illuminated by some light source, such as the Sun or a lamp.

Objects reflect, absorb, and scatter that light. Some of the reflected photons travel toward the camera.

Sun / lamp
    ↓
  photons
    ↓
   object
    ↓ reflected photons
    ↓
  camera

The important thing is that different parts of the scene send different amounts and wavelengths of light toward the camera.

For example:

A white object reflects lots of visible light.
A black object reflects relatively little.
A red object reflects more red wavelengths than blue/green wavelengths.
2. The camera lens collects and focuses the photons

The photons enter the camera's lens.

The lens bends the incoming rays so that photons coming from a particular 3D point in the world are focused onto a particular location on the image sensor.

Conceptually:

       object
          *
         / \
        /   \
       /     \
      /       \
   [    LENS    ]
         \   /
          \ /
       SENSOR

This is why the camera can convert a 3D scene into a 2D image.

The pinhole camera model from your UCLA notes simplifies this process by treating the aperture as a single point.

3. Photons hit the image sensor

Behind the lens is a silicon image sensor, typically a CMOS sensor in modern cameras.

The sensor contains millions of tiny photosensitive elements called pixels/photosites.

For example:

┌───┬───┬───┬───┐
│   │   │   │   │
├───┼───┼───┼───┤
│   │   │   │   │
├───┼───┼───┼───┤
│   │   │   │   │
└───┴───┴───┴───┘

Each photosite receives photons.

More photons generally produce a larger electrical signal.

So, roughly:

$$ \boxed{\text{more photons} \rightarrow \text{more electrons} \rightarrow \text{larger signal}} $$

This is where the physical world starts becoming an electrical measurement.

4. Photons generate electrons

This is the physics part.

When photons hit the semiconductor, they can transfer their energy to electrons through the photoelectric effect.

The sensor accumulates charge.

Imagine two pixels:

Dark region              Bright region

   few photons              many photons
       ↓                        ↓
   ○ ○ ○ ○                 ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○
       ↓                        ↓
   few electrons             many electrons
       ↓                        ↓
   small signal              large signal

So at this point we haven't created an image file.

We have millions of tiny electrical measurements.

5. The sensor measures color

There's another important complication.

A typical silicon photosite doesn't inherently tell you:

"I received 37% red, 42% green, and 21% blue."

It primarily measures the amount of light reaching it.

To obtain color, many cameras put a color filter array over the sensor.

The most common arrangement is the Bayer pattern:

G R G R
B G B G
G R G R
B G B G

Each photosite measures predominantly one color.

So one location might measure red, another green, another blue.

This means the RAW sensor data isn't normally an RGB image yet.

It's more like:

R G G R G B G R ...
G B R G B G R G ...

with each position containing a measured sensor value.

6. The analog electrical signal is read out

The sensor now has millions of analog electrical signals.

These signals have to be read from the sensor.

There can also be things like:

amplification
exposure/gain adjustments
black-level offsets
noise

At this point we're still dealing with analog electrical quantities.

7. The ADC converts the analog signal into numbers

Now comes the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC).

This is where your earlier question about 12-bit DN becomes important.

Suppose the ADC is 12-bit.

It can represent:

$$ 2^{12}=4096 $$

different values:

$$ 0,\ldots,4095 $$

So the analog sensor signal gets quantized into a number.

For example:

small signal  → DN = 217
medium signal → DN = 1832
large signal  → DN = 3901

These are digital numbers (DNs).

Now we finally have digital data.

8. We now have RAW sensor data

At this point, imagine the camera produces something conceptually like:

Pixel       Measurement
------------------------
(0,0)       1832
(1,0)        924
(2,0)       2103
(3,0)        451
...

These numbers correspond to sensor measurements.

This is approximately what RAW data represents.

Notice that we still don't have the normal RGB image you see on your computer.

We have:

$$ \boxed{\text{sensor measurements}} $$
9. Image processing turns RAW measurements into an image

Now the camera or computer performs a series of processing steps.

A simplified pipeline is:

RAW
 ↓
black-level correction
 ↓
noise / sensor corrections
 ↓
white balance
 ↓
demosaicing
 ↓
color correction
 ↓
tone mapping
 ↓
gamma / transfer function
 ↓
RGB image

Let's unpack the important ones.

10. Demosaicing reconstructs RGB

Remember:

G R G R
B G B G
G R G R
B G B G

Each photosite only measured one color.

But we want every final pixel to have:

$$ (R,G,B) $$

So the software estimates the missing color components using neighboring measurements.

For example:

RAW:

     R
   G   G
     B

       ↓ demosaicing

RGB pixel:

(R, G, B)

There are many sophisticated demosaicing algorithms.

This is one reason a RAW image isn't simply "three channels of 12-bit RGB."

11. White balance adjusts the colors

Different light sources have different color temperatures.

For example:

sunlight
fluorescent lighting
incandescent lighting

The camera needs to compensate so that something that is physically white appears white.

Conceptually:

$$ R' = aR $$ $$ G' = bG $$ $$ B' = cB $$

where the gains \(a,b,c\) compensate for the illumination.

12. Color correction maps sensor colors to a standard color space

The camera's sensor doesn't necessarily have exactly the same color response as the RGB color space used for displaying images.

So the camera applies a color transformation.

Conceptually:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} R'\\ G'\\ B' \end{bmatrix} = M \begin{bmatrix} R\\ G\\ B \end{bmatrix} $$

where \(M\) is some color correction matrix.

Eventually the image can be represented in a standard color space such as sRGB.

13. The image gets mapped to an 8-bit representation

Now we're getting close to what your computer normally works with.

A typical display-oriented image may have:

$$ R,G,B \in [0,255] $$

So each pixel is:

Pixel (100,200):

R = 237
G = 181
B = 92

That means the pixel is approximately an orange/yellow color.

Your image is now essentially:

$$ I(u,v) = (R,G,B) $$

where:

\(u,v\) = pixel location
\(R,G,B\) = color values
14. The image may be compressed

If you're saving a JPEG:

RGB image
    ↓
JPEG compression
    ↓
JPEG file

JPEG is lossy, meaning some information is deliberately discarded to make the file smaller.

RAW generally preserves much more of the original sensor information.

That's why:

RAW
 ↓
more sensor information
 ↓
larger file

while:

JPEG
 ↓
processed + compressed
 ↓
smaller file
15. Your computer reads the image file

Suppose you open a JPEG.

Your operating system/application decodes the JPEG back into pixel values:

Pixel 0: (124, 82, 51)
Pixel 1: (127, 84, 53)
Pixel 2: (131, 87, 54)
...

Now you have an RGB image in memory.

16. The GPU sends the pixels to the display

This is the final part people often forget.

Your CPU/GPU doesn't literally send "photons" from the image file to the monitor.

The GPU takes the RGB values and generates an electronic signal for the display.

For example:

Image memory
     ↓
    GPU
     ↓
display signal
     ↓
monitor pixels
17. The monitor converts electrical signals back into light

Each display pixel contains subpixels.

For a conventional RGB display:

┌─────────────────────┐
│  RED │ GREEN │ BLUE │
└─────────────────────┘

The monitor controls the intensity of each subpixel.

For example:

R = 255
G = 100
B = 20

means:

RED   → very bright
GREEN → moderately bright
BLUE  → low

The subpixels emit light.

Those photons enter your eyes.

Your visual system then interprets that light as a color and brightness.

The entire pipeline

                  (12/14 bit)

And this connects directly to the camera model you've been studying:

$$ \boxed{\text{3D world}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{optics}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{2D sensor location}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{pixel measurement}} $$

The camera model primarily describes the geometric part, such as how a 3D point ends up at pixel \((u,v)\). The sensor/electronics pipeline describes how much light at that location ultimately becomes the DN/RGB value.

That's the fundamental distinction:

$$ \boxed{\text{Geometry answers "where?"}} $$ $$ \boxed{\text{Photometry answers "how much light?"}} $$

And a digital image needs both.
